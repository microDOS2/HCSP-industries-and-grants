§4 · The field

A forty-year graveyard, and four organizations still standing

Commercial SCWO has a long casualty list built on corrosion and salt plugging — which is exactly why the survivors' operating records, and their finances, carry the evidentiary weight.

As of 2021 only about six commercial SCWO plants operated worldwide; a 2019 review found all commercially constructed plants except two had closed — mainly on corrosion, plugging, and economics. The flow below follows the field from the 1980s pioneers to today's four verifiable commercial operators. Dashed spans mark entities whose dates are graded estimates rather than documented; the ✕ endpoints are the lesson.

Only four organizations operate verifiable commercial-scale SCWO today. 374Water (NASDAQ: SCWO), a Duke University spinout and the closest analogue to the HCSP, sells the containerized AirSCWO 6 — yet its FY2025 annual report to the SEC (Form 10-K) shows revenue of $215,037 against a $21.0M net loss. General Atomics fields PERSES, the established supplier: commercial since ~2012, more than 6 million gallons destroyed, EPA-verified, but with a mixed reliability record. Aquarden (Denmark) sells the all-electric SuperOx — the field's all-electric benchmark. Revive Environmental operates Battelle's PFAS Annihilator as North America's first fully permitted commercial-scale SCWO destruction service for PFAS "forever chemicals".

Existing incineration suppliers are the real competitor. Roughly 24 licensed US hazardous-waste incinerators (RCRA combustors) run at 87–92% utilization with one new unit permitted in three decades and 5–7%/yr price escalation. HCSP does not need to beat their price by a little; the no-air-permit moat and on-site liability elimination let it sell speed and certainty while pricing at $2–5/gal beneath a $5–50/gal umbrella.

    §4C · Customers & global footprint

    Who actually pays them — and where in the world they really operate

    Named customers, hosts, and programs with locations, values, and status — read from SEC filings, US government spending records, state program records, and government publications, not from booth banners. Blue chips mark each operator's largest engagement, ink chips its smallest, with the precision caveats attached. Every row drills to its dated source.

    The pattern underneath the tables: every 374Water dollar of booked revenue traces to a government entity; General Atomics' realized footprint spans six countries but every current US deployment is government-funded; Aquarden's only multi-million-euro engagement is booked through its prime contractor, ArianeGroup; and Revive's entire destruction capacity sits in two Midwest facilities.

    The geographic coverage matrix — verified footprint vs. marketing reach

    Markers distinguish a verified operating footprint from time-boxed demos, announced-but-unbuilt deals, and outright marketing claims. Aquarden's partner-quoted "deployed in more than five countries" and Revive's "available in parts of Canada" have no named installation or customer behind them; 374Water's international horizon is aspiration with no country, partner, or timeline. Every cell drills to its evidence.

    What the map says

    All four survivors are regional players with government-heavy customer bases. 374Water is US-only across eight states and every booked dollar is municipal, state, or federal. GA's six-country realized footprint is real but rides entirely on government programs, UN environmental aid, and one unverified 2018 China sale — there is no PERSES-specific overseas channel. Aquarden's verifiable fleet is two French units plus its own Danish test plant. Revive is US-only with destruction centralized in Columbus, Ohio and Grand Rapids, Michigan, and all waste freighted to those two nodes.

    No competitor runs a true global small-facility channel — and that sharpens the scale-gap thesis. The gap below ≈$1M is not a regional accident that a stronger export program will close: it is structural in all four business models. Nobody sells a small facility anything it can buy and run itself, in any geography — and the two players with any international reach (GA via aid programs, Aquarden via ArianeGroup) reach foreign customers only through large channels run by governments or prime contractors.

    The words we use — a plain-English glossary
    SCWO
    Supercritical water oxidation — a way of destroying waste with very hot, high-pressure water.
    PFAS
    "Forever chemicals" — man-made chemicals that barely break down in nature.
    COD
    Chemical oxygen demand — a measure of how much organic waste is in the water.
    AFFF
    Firefighting foam — a major source of PFAS pollution.
    Autothermal
    The system makes its own heat from the waste it destroys.
    CapEx / OpEx
    Purchase cost / running cost.
    TAM / SAM / SOM
    Total market / reachable market / what we could win.
    TRL
    Technology readiness level — how proven the technology is, on a 1–9 scale.
    WDS
    Waste destruction service — we run the destruction for you.
    ZLD
    Zero liquid discharge — a rule that no liquid waste may leave a site.
    Skid
    A factory-built machine delivered on a steel frame — you hook it up and switch it on.
    Leachate
    The contaminated liquid that drains out of a landfill.
    Biosolids
    Sewage sludge — the solid leftovers of wastewater treatment.
    MCL
    The legal limit for a chemical in drinking water.
    ELG
    Federal limits on what a factory may discharge in its wastewater.
    CERCLA
    The US Superfund cleanup law — it decides who pays to clean up pollution.
    NOFO
    A grant application notice — the government announcing money you can apply for.
    SBIR / STTR
    US small-business research grant programs.
    SRF
    State water-infrastructure loan funds — cheap public loans for water projects.
    Refrigeration ton
    A standard measure of cooling capacity; 500 tons ≈ 1.8 MW of cooling.
