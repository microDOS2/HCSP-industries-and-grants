§0 · Executive summary

Buyers pay to eliminate liability, not to buy water

HCSP — a modular, 10 m³/day supercritical water oxidation system — is a destruction business whose byproduct happens to be distilled water, and the industries that gain the most from it are those that pay the most to make a regulated liability disappear.

Scored on six criteria across fifteen candidate industries, the ranking is led by PFAS destruction and landfill leachate (8.0/10), defense, maritime and remote installations (7.5), and India's zero-liquid-discharge textile clusters (7.4) — with distillery and ethanol vinasse the standout v2 entrant at 7.0 — while indoor agriculture, the anchor market of the originating technical briefing, scores only 4.2, twelfth of fifteen. The organizing fact is the energy model: per the manufacturer briefing, the HCSP is self-fueling once started — it generates hydrogen fuel on demand from a portion of the process water (electrolysis → clean H₂/O₂ combustion → SCWO heat), so net operating energy cost is negligible, and a working prototype exists. The economics therefore turn not on buying energy but on what the outputs are worth — and recovered water earns only ~$3.5/m³. No water sale can carry the business; only destruction-priced streams can.

HCSP's energy cost — negligible in operation per the manufacturer (self-fueling loop; grid power covers only the 75–95 kW startup/auxiliary spec) — sits far below incumbent destruction pricing — $5–10/gal for PFAS incineration, up to $28/gal for hazardous landfilling of firefighting foam, $15–50+/gal for halogenated solvents. Consolidated serviceable demand across viable verticals is ~$9–20B per year — roughly double the v1 reading once distillery, CAFO, island, and mining pools are added — with the widest gross addressable savings on halogenated solvents and PFAS concentrates (net payback pending the manufacturer cost model; HCSP has no published cost benchmark). And v2 adds a second organizing fact: past ~35–40 g/L COD and hub scale, the energy line inverts — the self-fueling plant starts exporting power. Both facts are contingent on third-party publication of prototype long-run data on salty feeds. Salt deposition is the documented failure mode of conventional SCWO designs — one HCSP answers by design, combusting hydrogen above 3,000 °F directly in the reactor so deposition never starts; the manufacturer reports no salt issues, and the residual item is a constructive validation agenda, not a red flag.

The v3 addendum re-ranks the same fifteen industries through a cost-savings lens — incumbent remediation cost per gallon against HCSP's negligible net energy cost (manufacturer-stated, self-fueling loop — no $/gal figure is attached to HCSP while its cost model is pending), the primary framing while manufacturer pricing remains TBD. Under that lens the ladder is led by halogenated solvents at $15–50+/gal and defense AFFF at $5–28/gal; PFAS concentrates hold top-3 under both lenses — the report's most robust finding — and the CEA hauling-captive niche jumps from #12 to #6. The same lens demotes distillery vinasse and textiles: their incumbents cost ~1¢/gal, so they are regulation-and-energy plays, not savings plays. The qualifier that travels with every savings figure: margin × volume = pool.

The top of the ranking is not a technology verdict but an economics verdict: every Tier 1 and Tier 2 market pairs a concentrated feed with an incumbent disposal price one to two orders of magnitude above HCSP's negligible net energy cost (self-fueling loop; manufacturer cost model pending — no $/gal attached).

§1 · Three facts that decide everything

The physics screen, the permitting moat, and the self-fueling loop

Before any industry ranking, three properties of the machine itself bound the addressable market.

60–150 g/L

The autothermal threshold

Above ~120–150 g/L COD the reaction heats itself; below it, SCWO is an energy sink.

Oxidizing organics releases heat. If the feed carries enough chemical energy — roughly ≥2 MJ/kg, equivalent to ~120–150 g/L COD — SCWO approaches autothermal operation; below that threshold the preheat duty falls on the machine's self-fueling hydrogen loop rather than the feed itself. The v2 LANL modeling refines the picture into a crossover (§3B): with heat recovery and a power cycle, net electricity crosses zero near ~35–40 g/L COD, meaningful export begins above ~60 g/L, and at 150 g/L the plant exports ~60 kWhₑ per cubic meter. This one variable predicts success or failure in every market examined — concentrated streams clear it directly or via an upstream separator; dilute streams sit 10–100× below it. The natural product form is therefore a concentrate-and-destroy train, not direct purification of dilute wastewater.

No air permit

The hidden economic moat

A sealed system emitting water vapor bypasses a permitting wall that incumbents cannot.

US hazardous-waste incineration is capacity-constrained: roughly 24 permitted RCRA combustors running at 88–92% utilization, one new unit permitted in three decades, and sustained 5–7%/yr price escalation. The sealed design — the only gaseous stream is vented excess oxygen — requires no air permit, a structural cost and schedule advantage over every thermal-destruction asset that must run a multi-year permitting gauntlet. Quantifying permitting time-to-operation is part of every sale.

Self-fueling

The on-demand hydrogen fuel loop

Once started, the machine generates its own fuel from process water — negligible net energy cost in operation.

The briefing powers the reactor by electrolyzing a portion of the process water on demand and combusting the resulting clean H₂/O₂ for SCWO process heat — a self-fueling loop: fuel is generated as needed, never purchased or stored in bulk, and net energy cost in operation is negligible once started. A working prototype exists. Grid electricity remains relevant only to the startup/auxiliary draw (75–95 kW spec), and the energy analysis in this report concerns the value of exported energy on concentrated feeds — not an operating cost.

The specification, as briefed

The HCSP (Hydro-Combustion Supercritical Purifier) is described in a technical briefing submitted to the California State Water Resources Control Board in August 2025, initially positioned for indoor-agriculture wastewater. The chemistry is settled science. SCWO holds the strongest third-party destruction record of any emerging PFAS technology: 99.9993% destruction-and-removal efficiency on firefighting foam in a US Army Corps of Engineers ERDC evaluation, >99.9% across four concentrated streams in a DIU/ESTCP demonstration, and >99.9999% on chemical-weapons hydrolysate over ~5,800 NRC-reviewed operating hours. Nothing in this report's risk assessment questions destruction efficacy — the risks lie entirely in reliability engineering and energy economics.

§2 · The machine

One skid, four stages, past the critical point

Pressurize to 250 bar, heat past 374 °C, and water becomes a single-phase fluid in which organics and oxygen are fully miscible — and oxidize completely in seconds to minutes. What leaves is distilled water, carbon dioxide, and a contained, inert sludge.

Per the briefing, the machine runs four stages: electrolysis of a portion of the process water on demand → combustion of the clean H₂/O₂ so generated, supplying process heat → supercritical water oxidation of the feed → separation into distilled water, CO₂, and inert sludge. Once started, the fuel loop is self-fueling — hydrogen is generated on demand from the process water itself, at negligible net energy cost — and a working prototype exists. SCWO does not concentrate, adsorb, or transfer pollutants — it mineralizes them. Above the critical point, carbon-hydrogen-oxygen organics oxidize to carbon dioxide and water; heteroatoms emerge as inorganic acids or salts; nothing combustible remains. The chart below draws the process the way an engineer would: as a path through the pressure–temperature plane, crossing the critical point into the supercritical region where the reactor operates — with the self-fueling hydrogen loop annotated where it attaches.

The second physical constraint is materials. Salts soluble in feed water become nearly insoluble in supercritical water, precipitating on reactor walls and causing plugging, heat-transfer loss, and under-deposit corrosion; the 2019–2024 literature consensus is that an economical salt-management solution has not yet been found for conventional preheater designs. The casualty list — HydroProcessing, Chematur's AquaCat, Hanwha, Innoveox, the Blue Grass program — is why feed screening is mandatory, and why the field's history is told in §4 as a flow of fates, not a list of vendors.

HCSP's design answer is to attack the deposition mechanism directly: instead of preheating the feed ahead of the reactor, it combusts hydrogen — generated on demand from the process water itself — at above 3,000 °F (≈1,650 °C) inside the reactor, so the walls never see the cold, salt-dropping zone that plugged the historical plants. The manufacturer reports no salt issues, and the approach has research footing: in-reactor hydrothermal flames are a documented salt-management strategy in the published SCWO literature. What remains is constructive, not cautionary: third-party publication of prototype long-run data on salty feeds — the validation agenda item that converts a manufacturer-stated design answer into bankable evidence.

§3 · The economics

Grid-price context, pricing umbrella, savings cliff

The investment case rests on one sentence: it is a waste-destruction business whose economics are set by disposal prices, not water prices.

Geography moves HCSP's economics through exactly two variables: the local disposal baseline it displaces, and the local grid power price — which prices the startup/auxiliary draw and sets the value of every exported kWh. Because the HCSP is self-fueling once started (per the manufacturer briefing), these prices are context, not an operating-cost floor: the same 75–95 kW startup/auxiliary spec reads as $10.8/m³-equivalent in Saudi Arabia or Texas and $55.7/m³-equivalent in Germany — a 5× swing in what auxiliary power costs and exported energy earns. Grid price also benchmarks the byproduct: the ~$3.5/m³ recovered-water value confirms water sales alone cannot carry the business — destruction pricing and energy export do.

§3B · Scale & energy — the v2 chapters

The scale ladder, the crossover, and the trigeneration stack

v1 priced a 10 m³/day module and stopped there. v2 asks the two questions that decide whether HCSP is a skid business or an infrastructure business: what does scale do to cost, and at what feed strength does the energy balance invert?

The ladder has four rungs — 10, 100, 1,000, and 1,900 m³/day. Two effects move down it together: capital cost per cubic meter follows a scaling exponent of n ≈ 0.5–0.7 per train, and net energy collapses from 130–250 kWh/m³ at module scale to 35–88 kWh/m³ at hub scale as heat-exchanger area outgrows duty. What scale does to fully loaded cost is withheld: earlier $/m³ figures came from a modeled stack, not the manufacturer, and are pending the manufacturer cost model. One honesty marker rides on the chart: the largest single-train SCWO plant ever built processed 306 t/d, and both of the largest plants ever built are defunct. Everything above that line is interpolated design economics, and the report prices the ladder on the pessimistic parallel-train branch accordingly.

Scale moves the intercept; feed strength moves the slope. The LANL integrated model is the cleanest statement of the crossover: with heat recovery and a Rankine power cycle, net electricity falls from +18 kWhₑ/m³ imported to −60 kWhₑ/m³ exported as feed rises from zero to ~160 g/L COD. Net-zero sits near ~35–40 g/L; meaningful export — ≥10 kWhₑ/m³ — begins above ~60 g/L. The module without a power cycle approaches the line and never crosses it. The stream rail below the chart shows why the league table sorts the way it does: brewery effluent, fab bulk water, and most produced water sit structurally below the line; dewatered biosolids, Brazilian vinasse, and molasses spent wash sit squarely above it.

Above the crossover, the plant becomes a small utility, and the question shifts from cost to revenue stacking. At 1,000 m³/day on 100 g/L feed, oxidation releases 16.1 MW_th of gross reaction heat — but ~60% of it is spoken for: preheating the feed is the price of autothermal operation. The exportable surplus, 9.6 MW_th (6.4–11.2 across the 70–85% heat-recovery band), cascades through an extraction turbine into 1.4 MWₑ of power and 8.2 MW_th of 150–180 °C heat into 4.9 MW_c of absorption cooling (~1,392 refrigeration tons) — worth ≈$1.15M/yr against $0.79M/yr for an all-cooling configuration. The hierarchy beneath the cascade is the strategy: power earns $15–20 per MWh_th, cooling only ~$10 — unless chilled water sells at district tariffs of $30–48, in which case cooling leapfrogs power and data centers become the anchor offtaker.

The backup-power verdict: LFP by default

One v2 engineering chapter settles a component question the hard way. Nickel-iron batteries carry a century of durability legend — but standby duty is exactly where their 20–30%/month self-discharge is disqualifying: a site dark for six weeks arrives at the emergency with a flat battery. LFP wins every row that governs UPS and black-start duty, most by an order of magnitude, at $70/kWh pack prices.

The decision rule. Specify LFP as the standard UPS/black-start battery on every unit — default bill of materials, not a site option. NiFe only where the unit is daily-cycled off-grid, ambient extremes are sustained, and replacement logistics dominate lifecycle cost — and even then as a hybrid with a small LFP string for instant black-start. Underwrite absorption cooling at field-measured COP (0.28–0.45 on waste heat), never the design figure.

§4 · The field

A forty-year graveyard, and four organizations still standing

Commercial SCWO has a long casualty list built on corrosion and salt plugging — which is precisely why the survivors' operating records, and their finances, carry the evidentiary weight.

As of 2021 only about six commercial SCWO plants operated worldwide; a 2019 review found all commercially constructed plants except two had closed — mainly on corrosion, plugging, and economics. The flow below follows the field from the 1980s pioneers to today's four verifiable commercial operators. Dashed spans mark entities whose dates are graded estimates rather than documented; the ✕ endpoints are the lesson.

Only four organizations operate verifiable commercial-scale SCWO today. 374Water (NASDAQ: SCWO), a Duke University spinout and the closest analogue to the HCSP, sells the containerized AirSCWO 6 — yet its FY2025 Form 10-K shows revenue of $215,037 against a $21.0M net loss. General Atomics fields PERSES, the incumbent: commercial since ~2012, more than 6 million gallons destroyed, EPA-verified, but with a mixed reliability record. Aquarden (Denmark) sells the all-electric SuperOx — the field's all-electric benchmark. Revive Environmental operates Battelle's PFAS Annihilator as North America's first fully permitted commercial-scale SCWO PFAS destruction service.

The incineration incumbency is the real competitor. Roughly 24 permitted US RCRA combustors run at 87–92% utilization with one new unit permitted in three decades and 5–7%/yr price escalation. HCSP does not need to beat their price by a little; the no-air-permit moat and on-site liability elimination let it sell speed and certainty while pricing at $2–5/gal beneath a $5–50/gal umbrella.

    §5 · The verdict

    A real market, gated by reliability — and by dates on a calendar

    The opportunity is real but not yet banked. The dominant risk is execution under HCSP's own control — uptime, feed discipline, and publishing the prototype data behind its salt-free combustion design — not market existence.

    The scale below weighs the two cases across the fifteen-industry v2 ranking. On the right pan, the demand-side evidence: federally validated destruction, a $5–28/gal incumbent umbrella, the no-air-permit moat, dated regulatory clocks — now including India's CPCB distillery-ZLD mandate — and the v2 finding that scale and feed strength invert the energy line into export. On the left, the supply-side record: a 30-year graveyard, a pre-scale listed leader, a downgraded market estimate, and the new v2 weight — no SCWO train above 306 t/d has ever operated, so hub economics remain interpolated until a rung is proven. The dashed weights hovering above are the upgrade triggers, and the hatched seal at the base carries the falsification lines. The reading is qualitative, not a score.

    The per-industry call

    Go where the stream is concentrated and the baseline is destruction-priced; hold where compulsion is regional; walk away where the physics forecloses. The tiers, not the decimal points, should drive resource allocation.

    The demand clock

    Several of the report's risks and catalysts are dated and therefore tradeable: EPA's guidance revision, the October 2026 AFFF deadline, the landfill ELG window, and the 2031 compliance horizon all carry known clocks, allowing scenario-triggered capital deployment rather than blind staging.

    Eight recommendations (abbreviated, v2)

    • 1 · Publish the self-fueling energy balance with prototype data — the on-demand hydrogen loop (fuel generated from process water, negligible net energy cost once started) is a differentiator; independent documentation converts it from claim to diligence asset.
    • 2 · Lead with waste-destruction-as-a-service at $2–5/gal, using equipment sales only for high-credit industrial buyers — pharma, fabs, distilleries.
    • 3 · Gate every opportunity on feed concentration — the v2 crossover refines the screen: net-zero near 35–40 g/L COD with power recovery, export above ~60 g/L — and keep the four sub-4.5 verticals (CEA, CAFO, mining, data centers) out of the equipment pipeline.
    • 4 · Quantify permitting time-to-operation in every sale, monetizing the air-permit moat against capacity-constrained incineration.
    • 5 · Retain indoor agriculture strictly as a regulatory showcase under California's zero-waste invitation; never make CEA revenue load-bearing.
    • 6 · Publish the produced-water model on independently benchmarked inputs — the Marcellus-hub case prices the niche against documented disposal economics ($3–15/bbl trucking corridor), not basin-scale placeholder figures; its IRR/payback is restated only once the manufacturer cost model lands.
    • 7 · Build the scale ladder as parallel trains, not giant vessels — accept the weaker n ≈ 0.85 exponent for replicated, proven pressure envelopes; stage-gate each rung on the availability data of the rung below.
    • 8 · Site trigeneration plants against anchor chilled-water buyers — data centers as offtakers, not hosts: the plant sits at the waste (wastewater plant, distillery, F&B complex) and sells power, cooling, and reclaimed water across the fence to campuses facing 4–7-year grid queues. Never build one without a ≥500 RT contracted offtaker.

    §8 · Funding landscape — Part III

    Climate-era money is contracting; PFAS money is at its all-time peak

    The addressable grant universe for a wastewater-destruction technology vendor is smaller than it was in 2024 — but better targeted. The programs that died were ones HCSP was unlikely to win anyway; the money that survived concentrates in PFAS destruction, water reuse, and defense resilience — precisely HCSP's value proposition. The strategic error to avoid is writing proposals to dead programs; the strategic opportunity is that PFAS funding is counter-cyclical.

    Four structural events define the year. First, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA, P.L. 119-21, July 4, 2025) rescinded unobligated IRA climate-justice appropriations, and EPA terminated 781 environmental justice grants and closed the Office of Environmental Justice entirely — dead as funding sources, and never a fit for a for-profit technology company anyway. Second, the Department of Energy's demonstration arm is gone: the Office of Clean Energy Demonstrations was abolished in the November 2025 reorganization after terminating 24 awards (~$3.7B) in May 2025 and roughly $7.56B more across DOE offices in October 2025. There is no successor office and no reason to plan around one.

    Third, two programs were interrupted and restored. FEMA's BRIC program — cancelled in April 2025 — was court-restored, and a combined FY2024–25 NOFO for $1B closed July 23, 2026; BRIC funds states and localities, so HCSP is at most an indirect vendor partner. More consequentially, SBIR/STTR lapsed 6.5 months and were reauthorized on April 13, 2026 through September 30, 2031, with higher ceilings and a new "Strategic Breakthrough" Phase II worth up to $30M at large agencies — the most reliable direct-apply channel for a company of HCSP's profile is fully restarted. Fourth, the BIL's PFAS-dedicated water money is in its final appropriation year: the last $1B of the $5B EC-SDC program (May 2026), a reaffirmed $4B of DWSRF PFAS money, and the closing $1B CWSRF Emerging Contaminants supplement — which covers wastewater, stormwater, and landfill leachate.

    The counterweight — and the thesis of this layer — is that PFAS-specific money is expanding even as general climate money contracts. The 3M public-water-system settlement alone has paid out more than $3.5B to Phase 1 claimants, with installments running through 2033–2036; Tyco ($750M) and BASF ($316.5M) payouts began in March–April 2026. New Jersey's court-approved PFAS Abatement Fund (August 7, 2026) stands up a $795M state vehicle with at least $150M routed to POTWs. Wisconsin's Acts 200/201 release more than $130M, including direct grants to landfills for on-site leachate treatment. The market signal is unambiguous: destruction technology is moving from pilot novelty to funded line item. HCSP's task is to be specified, not just eligible.

    §9 · The grant register

    Ninety-two programs, screened — and three doors into them

    Roughly 90% of the dollar volume relevant to HCSP flows to utilities, municipalities, and public systems — not to technology vendors. Winning this money is therefore primarily a business-development exercise (getting HCSP written into other entities' applications), not a grant-writing exercise.

    Pursuit resources should be organized around three access modes, in descending order of dollar potential. Mode (b) — named technology in a partner's application — is where the big money is, and the template transaction already exists: the City of Orlando secured a federal BIL grant through Florida's CWSRF (~$800K, February 2024) to fund an SCWO demonstration by 374Water at its Iron Bridge facility; that demonstration converted into a five-year waste-destruction-services license (March 2026) and then a $3M–$5M full-scale PFAS destruction hub (June 2026). The pattern repeats at OC San (6-ton/day SCWO through the standing RISE program) and in Minnesota (an ENRTF-funded SCWO biosolids project, December 2025). The lesson: the grant proposal is written by the utility; the vendor's job is to supply the engineering package, cost data, and performance evidence that make the application winnable.

    Mode (a) — direct-apply programs — are fewer but actionable now: Reclamation's DWPR, the ONR Long-Range BAA, SERDP/ESTCP, all SBIR/STTR vehicles, NYSERDA's waste-to-fuels solicitation, PA PENNVEST (unusually, private owner/operators are eligible), AZ WIFA augmentation project teams, and USDA B&I loan guarantees. Mode (c) — vendor/subrecipient to prime contractors — monetizes demand HCSP cannot touch directly: DoD's AFFF stockpile disposal is executed through installation-level contracts with prime environmental contractors, and DoD's 2024–25 demonstration series concluded on-site destruction beats off-site disposal. Allocate roughly 40% of pursuit effort to Mode (b), 35% to Mode (a), 25% to Mode (c): the direct applications generate credibility; the partnerships generate dollars.

    Closed doors — the candid no-go list

    Killing pursuits is as valuable as starting them. The following receive zero proposal effort, regardless of surface appeal:

    • USDA REAP grants — paused. No FY2026 NOFO; no grant awards until regulations are rewritten under EO 14315, and prior applicants without executed agreements must reapply. (Guaranteed loans remain open.)
    • EPA environmental justice programs — dead. 781 grants terminated, offices closed, unobligated funds rescinded by OBBBA; litigation is ongoing but these programs targeted community nonprofits, not technology vendors.
    • DOE OCED — abolished. No successor office, ~$11.3B in awards terminated, FY2026 request was $0; do not build a plan on revival.
    • 48C Advanced Energy Project Credit — closed. All $10B allocated across two rounds; OBBBA capped the program with no Round 3 expected.
    • Brewers Association / Distilled Spirits Council — no programs. Neither runs sustainability grants or cost-share pilots; distillery/brewery verticals must be funded by customer-paid pilots or state programs (e.g., CEC FPIP for food processors).
    • XPRIZE Water Scarcity ($119M) — closed to new entrants. Registration is shut, 37 semifinalists were named in May 2026, and the scope is seawater desalination, not wastewater destruction. Monitor future Mohamed bin Zayed Water Initiative competitions only.
    • India distillery ZLD finance — inaccessible to a US vendor. The interest-subvention scheme (~6% p.a. for five years) funds Indian distilleries' CPCB-certified zero-liquid-discharge capital, not foreign equipment makers; the only route is an Indian JV or EPC partnership, with Imagine H2O Asia as the regional on-ramp.
    • CalRecycle Organics Grant Program — excludes the core feedstock. Biosolids are explicitly ineligible; only landfill-diverted food/green waste qualifies, so the program cannot fund an HCSP biosolids project even though for-profits may apply.
    • Also-rans to deprioritize: DARPA (water program concluded; no current water/PFAS topics), DHS S&T SBIR (no water topics; one cycle per year), EPA STAR (for-profits ineligible as prime), and CA cannabis wastewater funding (no program exists).

    The common thread: every dead program is one where either the applicant type was wrong, the feedstock was wrong, or the politics moved. The pipeline should be re-screened against these three filters quarterly — the 2026–2027 environment is volatile enough that §8's list of the living and the dead will need a refresh by mid-2027.

    Stacking rules

    • Rule 2 · Stack vertically, never horizontally. The correct stack is sequential across project phases and non-overlapping across cost categories: a DWPR pilot ($800K federal) funds technology validation → a state grant or cost-share (WI Act 201, NJ Water Bank, CEC EPIC matching funds) funds the first commercial deployment → WIFIA debt (49% of eligible costs) finances scale-out at the utility host, with USDA B&I guarantees covering HCSP's own manufacturing expansion. Federal programs generally prohibit using one federal grant as the non-federal cost share for another on the same scope; cost share must come from state money, settlement money, customer payments, or private capital. Assume every application is audited on this point.
    • Rule 3 · One anchor utility per state program. SRF and EC funds flow through state priority lists; two HCSP-sponsored applications competing in the same state's Intended Use Plan dilute both. Pick one anchor host per state (a POTW with a biosolids/PFAS cost crisis, a landfill with ELG exposure, or a small water system with settlement cash), put HCSP in their application as the named technology, and replicate the Orlando conversion path: funded demonstration → service license → hub.

    Private & catalytic capital, disciplined. The genuinely non-dilutive private money is real but small: Imagine H2O's zero-equity accelerator and recoverable Pilot Fund, PRIME Coalition's recoverable grants for first-of-a-kind hardware, WRF's 1:1 Tailored Collaboration match (≤$150K), and Elemental Impact's DCII ($500K–$5M per project through 2027). Burnt Island, Microsoft CIF, Breakthrough Energy, and S2G are equity — not grants — and route to the capital-raise track. Hyperscaler water-replenishment programs are offtake revenue, not grants. Discipline about this distinction saves months of misdirected effort.
